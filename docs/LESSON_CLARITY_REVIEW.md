# Lesson clarity review (October 2026)

Scope: the English text of all 62 lessons: pages, observe tips, the common mistake, quiz explanations and Israel notes. The question was whether a beginner can follow each page, and what the smallest change is that makes it understandable without turning it into an essay. Four reviewers each read 16 lessons using the same checklist. Every lesson got a clarity score from 1 (hard) to 5 (clear for a beginner).

## Summary

- **Average clarity: 3.3 / 5.** 27 lessons score 4–5, 25 score 3 and 10 score 2.
- **The hands-on lessons read best:** marina (52–54), catamaran (61), night approach (62) and the early sail-trim lessons (10–15).
- **The rules and planning lessons read worst:** collision rules (55–60), coastal decisions (47–50), True and apparent wind (20), and Lines, winches and loads (5). Their text follows the rulebook too closely.
- **Most fixes are small:** about 20–70 extra words per lesson, roughly 3,200 words across the course. About 11 lessons need dense pages split, and 3 need restructuring (5, 20, 48).

## A short writing guide for the fixes

1. **Headline = the point.** The first sentence says what the page teaches, in plain words.
2. **Then what and why.** One sentence on what it is, one on why it matters or what goes wrong otherwise.
3. **Explain each term once per lesson**, in brackets or after a comma.
4. **No more than four items in a sentence.** Longer lists become bullets.
5. **Each number gets a picture.** For example: knots → walking pace; metres → boat lengths; degrees → compass points.
6. **Rules in plain words first**, rule number last.
7. **One app note at most**, as a single closing sentence starting "In the app, …".
8. **Keep the length:** a fixed page grows by no more than about 40%. If it would grow more, split it.

- **restructure:** reorder or rewrite the lesson.

## Example rewrites

These show the target style. Each fixes one page without much extra length.

**Lesson 20, True and apparent wind (page 1)**

> Before: Apparent wind is airflow relative to the yacht. Weather wind is relative to fixed ground; wind over water subtracts current from that air velocity. “True wind” may use either ground or water as its reference, so check the instrument convention.

> After: Apparent wind is the wind you feel on a moving boat: the real wind plus the breeze your own motion makes, like the headwind you feel cycling in still air. True wind is what you would feel standing still. Some instruments measure it over the ground, others over the water (they differ when there is current), so check which yours shows.

**Lesson 60, Restricted visibility and a complete encounter plan (page 4)**

> Before: Unless absence of collision risk has been determined, hearing a fog signal apparently forward of the beam, or being unable to avoid close quarters with a vessel forward of the beam, requires reducing speed to the minimum at which course can be kept. Take all way off if necessary and navigate with extreme caution until the danger is over.

> After: In fog, slow right down if you hear a fog signal somewhere ahead of you, or if a boat ahead is getting too close to avoid. Go only as fast as you need to keep steering, because you cannot yet tell where the other boat is going. Stop completely if needed, and stay very careful until the danger has passed. The only exception: you are already sure there is no risk of collision.

**Lesson 5, Lines, winches, and loads (page 3)**

> Before: The main and headsail hoists set how much sail is exposed. The two sheets change sail angle independently. The traveler moves the boom’s sheeting position; vang tension controls modeled twist, while the outhaul flattens the modeled sail foot. Change one control at a time and compare the response.

> After: The hoist controls set how far each sail is raised. Each sheet sets one sail's angle to the wind. Three more controls shape the mainsail. The traveler slides the mainsheet's lower end across the boat. The vang pulls the boom down, so the top of the sail does not twist open (Page 6). The outhaul stretches the bottom edge, which flattens the sail for strong wind. Change one control at a time, so you can see what each one does.

**Lesson 2, Safety gear aboard (page 5)**

> Before: When refuelling, stop the engine and put out any flame, close the hatches, and switch the automatic bilge pump off so it cannot pump spilt fuel into the sea. Keep absorbent rags ready, check that the tank breather is clear and watch the gauge so the tank does not overflow. If heavy flooding beats the bilge pumps, the engine’s sea-water cooling pump can also pump the bilge, on boats fitted to switch its intake.

> After: Refuelling is a fire risk, because fuel vapour is heavier than air and can collect inside the boat. Stop the engine, put out any flame and close the hatches. Switch off the automatic bilge pump (the pump that empties water from the bottom of the hull), so it cannot pump spilt fuel into the sea. Keep rags ready, check the tank's air vent is clear, and watch the gauge so the tank does not overflow. [Move the flooding tip to its own page.]

## What a rewrite would take

- **Reader support for short lists.** Page bodies are shown as one paragraph today. Lines starting with "• " should be shown as a list, which is a small change in `src/learning/reader.js`.
- **Optionally, a glossary.** Tapping a defined term would show its meaning. That would let pages stay short without explaining the same word again in every lesson.
- **Translation.** Each reworded page needs five new translations. Rewrite one module at a time in a single pass, using the same English-source and per-language agent process as the new lessons.
